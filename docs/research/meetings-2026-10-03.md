# Meetings: aivi as a participant — the body plan

Research 2026-10-03 (web sources as of this date), refined 2026-10-03 after
the operator's rulings: **Google Meet and Google Calendar go first** —
that's what the demo audience runs, and the demo is the point — the
**body is aivi's own virtual camera and virtual microphone with an audio
tap for listening**, and the design must serve **more than one meeting
platform** the way forge/tracker serve more than one code host.

## The body, in one paragraph

aivi does not scrape a meeting page and it does not beg each platform for
API permission. **aivi owns one real Chrome — the persistent
`aivi_browser` Chrome it already keeps — and that Chrome has a face and
a voice.** An init script, installed before any meeting page loads,
replaces the page's media devices: `getUserMedia` returns aivi's
streams (PCM out of the voice engine for the microphone, an animated
canvas for the camera), and the meeting's incoming audio — the remote
WebRTC tracks — is tapped in-page and streamed back for local
speech-to-text. No operating-system audio anywhere; the same code runs
on macOS and Linux. The meeting platforms see an ordinary participant;
what makes it aivi is behind the two streams.
Because the trick is below the platform, **one body works on every
platform with a web client** — Meet, Teams, Zoom, Webex, Jitsi — and a
platform adapter shrinks to: open this URL, click join, tell me
who's here, and stream me what the tab shows.

## Why this is the logical shape (and what it rests on)

1. **The mic-injection pattern is in production, in the open.** Several
   shipped projects do exactly this: the meeting page records from a
   fake/virtual microphone and a pump streams TTS PCM into it.
   `CoderOMaster/meeting-assistant` injects bot audio **directly into
   Chrome's mic stream** — in-page, with interruption handling and echo
   detection ("bot stops mid-sentence when a human speaks"), which is
   the whole hard part, already solved once. `NousResearch/hermes-agent`
   ships a Google Meet plugin end to end (Playwright joins, speaks a TTS
   PCM stream, auto-unmutes when Meet seats it muted) — and its issue
   tracker documents the live failure modes (joined-but-muted, the pump
   exiting early) for free.
2. **aivi already keeps the Chrome.** The browser plugin is one
   persistent Chrome for unattended sessions and shared logins, and
   Playwright's `connectOverCDP` attaches to an already-running Chrome
   with a signed-in profile — the open `screenappai/meeting-bot`
   (MIT, joins Meet/Teams/Zoom) supports exactly that attach mode. The
   signed-in real Chrome is also the field's answer to Meet's
   bot-detection (puppeteer-extra #334): a real profile over CDP, not a
   fresh headless signature.
3. **Listening is a track tap, not an OS tap.** The meeting's audio
   arrives in the page as **remote WebRTC tracks** — every voice-AI
   WebRTC client receives them the same way (`RTCPeerConnection`
   `track` events). Tap them into an `AudioWorklet`, stream PCM to the
   host, done. It hears the meeting and not the machine, it is the
   same code on every OS, and it needs no permission the browser page
   hasn't already been given. (An OS-level tap — macOS **Core Audio
   taps** (`sparticleinc/mac-audio-capture` npm; `AudioTee` the
   reference), Linux null-sink/PipeWire — exists and is filed for the
   one chapter that needs it: a *native* meeting app, below.)
4. **The in-page override needs nothing installed.** Before the page
   loads, an init script (`Page.addScriptToEvaluateOnNewDocument` /
   Playwright `addInitScript`) replaces `navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia`:
   audio returns a `MediaStream` from an `AudioContext` whose source is
   a WS-fed `AudioWorklet`; video returns `canvas.captureStream()`. The
   meeting page — Meet included — accepts them as its devices. This is
   the demo's whole media surface: **zero system installs, pure CDP.**

## The voice: ears and mouth, local, with a persistent identity

The host is Apple Silicon; MLX already serves aivi's models (the operator
has `mlx-serve` up). The audio stack has an all-local shape
(`mlx-audio` / `mlx-speech` / the VibeVoice MLX forks):

- **Mouth — VibeVoice.** MIT weights: 1.5B and 7B, plus
  **VibeVoice-Realtime-0.5B**, the streaming variant built for
  conversation latency. The operator's find — persisted voice identity —
  is first-class in the toolchain: encode a reference clip once
  (`--save-voice` → a `.safetensors` voice embedding) and synthesize
  with `--voice` after: bit-identical output, ~100 ms prefill (the
  EmZod fork caches the per-voice prefix KV and boots from a pre-quantized
  snapshot in ~3 s, and ships a **framed-stdio inference worker** built
  for a resident audio daemon — the exact sidecar shape). Voice files
  live in the home beside `.env`, 0600, never in config. Kokoro-82M is
  the instant fallback voice while VibeVoice warms. Languages — the
  mouth must speak Dutch as well as English — get their own section
  below.
- **Ears — local STT, streaming, with our own voice labels.**
  `mlx-whisper` or `parakeet-mlx` (fast, on-device) behind a silero-VAD
  (ONNX) segmenter. Speaker attribution is **ours, already working**
  (the operator's): diarization separates voices, a couple of manual
  assignments enroll a voiceprint, and the system remembers — the same
  voices are recognized in every later meeting. The labels survive any
  platform redesign because they were never the platform's.
- **Eyes.** A screen share is a rendered video track; **CDP tab
  screencast** (`Page.startScreencast`, a steady JPEG stream of the
  Meet tab) or a changed-what screenshot feeds a vision model — local
  MLX vision, a cloud one as an adapter. "Look at this chart" works,
  and shared frames land in the meeting record beside the transcript.
- **Plumbing.** MLX is Python, aivi is Node: a **sidecar child process
  over framed stdio** — PCM and small JSON in, PCM out — the same shape
  as the CLI's exec relay. The host never loads a model into its own
  process.

## Google first — the demo's pieces

**Calendar (Google Calendar API first).** `googleapis` npm; OAuth once,
refresh token in the home like every other secret. The live question —
"what's my next meeting", "join the 3pm" — is `events.list` with a
timeMin; the *reactive* answer is **push watch channels** (a channel
renews itself; renewal is a due job, not a timer — the no-polling rule
holds). `onlineMeetings: {conferenceData}` carries the join URL. Later,
the same role answers Microsoft Graph (`onlineMeetings` + webhook
subscriptions) and plain **CalDAV via `tsdav`** (polling with ctag —
which is a system job like `projects-sync`, so still no new timers).

**Meet (browser adapter, the first meeting adapter).** The flow, all
over CDP against the persistent Chrome:

1. `connectOverCDP` → new tab → the `meet.google.com/xxx` URL.
2. Init script installs the mic and camera streams (above).
3. Click join (the pre-join screen's "Join now"); Meet seats guests
   muted — `hermes` documents the unmute-after-admission dance.
4. **Listen — and label the voices ourselves. No captions.** The
   remote-track tap ships the meeting's PCM to the host; VAD segments
   the speech, local STT words it. Attribution is
   **our own voice-labelling** (built and working, the operator's):
   diarization separates the speakers, a **couple of manual
   assignments** — "that's Alice" — enroll a voiceprint, and the
   system remembers; every later meeting recognizes the same voices.
   No dependence on Meet's caption DOM, nothing to break when Meet
   redesigns, and the labels are ours to keep across meetings.
5. **Eyes.** In a call, a screen share is a video track; the page
   renders it. The cheap, robust way to *see* it: **CDP screencast of
   the tab** (`Page.startScreencast`, a steady JPEG stream of what
   Meet shows — the shared slide, the shared face) or a periodic tab
   screenshot when something changes, fed to a vision model (local
   MLX vision, or a cloud one as an adapter). Eyes on the meeting
   means: "aivi, look at this chart" works, the shared screen lands
   in the meeting record next to the transcript, and frames OCR what
   people put on screen instead of what they said. If Meet ever
   renders shared text as on-screen captions, our diarized audio plus
   frame OCR label it — an opportunistic extra, never a dependence.
6. **Speak**: text → VibeVoice worker → PCM over the WS → AudioWorklet →
   the mic stream. **Barge-in**: VAD on the tap pauses the pump mid-word
   when a human speaks (the meeting-assistant reference's behavior).
7. **Face**: the aivi robot — a low-poly GLB whose screen-face is a 2D
   canvas drawn as its emissive texture (state, eyes, a waveform mouth
   dancing to its own PCM), handed to the page as the camera stream.
   The webcam is free once the mic works, and it is half the wow.
   The design section below owns the details.
8. **Announce**: post "aivi is here, recording with its own voice —
   say 'aivi, …' to ask it something" to the meeting chat on join.
   Consent as a feature, never a footnote.

**The wow demo, end to end**: the 3pm is on the calendar → aivi joins on
time with its face and its persisted voice → the meeting talks; nobody
notices the mic is a worklet → someone says "aivi, what did we decide
about the schema?" → it answers *in the meeting*, in voice → after the
call the transcript lands in the conversation, dreaming turns the
decisions into `facts.md`, and the next morning aivi can say what
yesterday's meeting decided. The memory half is free: see roles.

**Transcripts are their own thing — a first-class artifact, not a
by-product** (ruled 2026-10-03). Each meeting keeps its record under
the home (one directory per meeting: the transcript, the participant
list, the calendar event it came from), and it is **not** dreaming's
throwaway scratch — dreaming's own transcripts are ephemeral; a
meeting's transcript is durable, the meeting's asset. Three consumers,
in order of nearness:

1. **In the meeting and right after**: aivi answers "what did we say
   about X?" from the transcript itself — exact words, attributed,
   quotable — not from a summary of a summary.
2. **Follow-up work reads it directly**: the action items, decisions
   and open questions in a transcript are what a post-meeting job turns
   into tickets (a tracker lane!), summary messages to participants,
   reminder jobs with due dates. The transcript is the source of truth
   the follow-up quotes, the same way a run's ledger is.
3. **Dreaming reviews it** along with conversations, so meeting
   decisions become `facts.md` and proposals like anything else said.

The meeting directory is a natural **knowledge source** — the `conversation`
kind already exists — so `aivi_search` reaches transcripts the same way
it reaches everything else said, with no new index machinery.

And the channel-events shape still holds *while the meeting runs*: the
live transcript arrives as channel-shaped lines (origin `meet`, the
meeting id as the channel, speaker-attributed), so the engine, identity
and commands ride along during the call. The durable transcript beside
it is what outlives the call.

## The generic roles — core spells them, adapters fill them

The forge/tracker move, one layer up. Nothing upstream says "Google".

| Role | What it answers | Adapters, in build order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **calendar** | when and where: upcoming events, join URLs, free/busy, "a meeting is starting" | `google` (API + watch channels) → `ms-graph` → `caldav` (`tsdav`, the suite-agnostic floor: Fastmail/Apple/Nextcloud/Google) |
| **meeting** | presence: join(url), transcript of who-said-what, say(text), leave, participants, what's on screen | `meet` (browser) → `teams-web` / `zoom-web` / `webex` / `jitsi` (same body, per-platform join and tab reader) → `zoom-rtms` (Zoom's official **Node.js Real-Time Media Streams SDK**: sanctioned live per-participant audio/video/transcripts over webhooks — the native door when a demo needs no browser) |
| **voice** | ears and mouth: stt(pcm)→text with speaker segments; tts(text, voiceId)→pcm | MLX sidecar (VibeVoice voices, whisper/parakeet ears) → a cloud STT/TTS as just another adapter |
| **channel** *(built)* | conversation: a meeting is a conversation **without threads** | the meeting transcript arrives as channel-shaped events — origin `meet`, the meeting id as the channel, speaker-attributed user lines |

The payoff line: a meeting arriving as channel events gets the
**ChannelEngine, link/identity, chat commands, and dreaming for free**.
"aivi knew the meeting happened and what was decided" needs no new
memory machinery — only the adapter's transcript emitter and the
meeting's durable transcript beside it.

Platform notes for the columns after Meet:

- **Zoom** is the friendly native one: official **RTMS Node SDK**
  (`rtms-quickstart-js`) delivers live per-participant audio, video,
  transcripts and events via webhooks; since 2026-03 cross-account joins
  require an authorized app (ZAK/OBF/RTMS). Raw audio **send** needs the
  native Meeting SDK (macOS/Windows/Linux C++) — or the body works on
  zoom.us web like it works on Meet.
- **Teams**' sanctioned live-media bot is .NET-on-Windows only
  (Microsoft Q&A confirms: no Node, REST or WebSocket path, no roadmap);
  Graph gives post-meeting transcripts in any language; the browser body
  is what every commercial Teams notetaker actually does during a call.
- **Webex** has a community Node SDK and a documented real-time media
  path through the browser SDK (Cisco's own blog builds a meeting bot
  that joins and reads media streams) — a fine third column.
- **Jitsi Meet** is the self-hosted dream: Apache-2.0, `lib-jitsi-meet`
  in Node joins a room as a participant with full track control — the
  body needs no override there, it *is* a participant. If a target
  company runs anything self-hosted, it's this.

## Build order

1. **Calendar adapter (Google)** + a `calendar.watch` system job —
   aivi knows meetings are coming. No media. A day.
2. **Meet join + listen**: CDP body, tap → VAD → STT, our voiceprint
   labels → transcript as channel events into the engine. The first "what did we decide?"
   moment. This is the demo's spine.
3. **Speak + barge-in**: VibeVoice worker, WS-fed AudioWorklet mic,
   VAD-pause. The wow lands here.
4. **Face**: the robot — a CC0 low-poly GLB with a canvas-drawn screen
   face (big eyes, state glyphs) behind `captureStream`, then the
   living layer: waveform mouth from its own PCM, blink cycles, the
   perk-up. A day for the first face on screen, days for the soul.
5. **Email channel** (`imapflow`/`mailparser`/`nodemailer`) — still the
   cheapest new channel overall and the identity bridge for meeting
   strangers (reply-to-aivi finds the transcript); the demo can wave it
   in later.
6. **Zoom adapter (RTMS)** — proves the meeting role on a sanctioned
   API, same seams, no browser.
7. Teams-web / Webex / Jitsi adapters as the demo circuit demands.

## How fancy can the face get

The face is a canvas; anything a canvas can draw, Meet can see. The
tiers, cheapest first — all of them drive the *same* `captureStream()`:

- **Tier 0 — the honest mark.** A drawn sigil or portrait that breathes
  (a slow scale/opacity cycle) and shifts colour by state: listening,
  thinking, speaking. An afternoon. Already convincing in its own way:
  it says *I am aivi* rather than trying to pass.
- **Tier 1 — a rigged 2D puppet with real lip-sync.** Audio-driven
  visemes in the browser are a solved, tiny thing: Meta's Oculus
  viseme set (15 visemes) has ONNX ports, and there are zero-dependency
  ~15 KB engines that make **any 2D browser avatar talk from streaming
  audio** with real-time viseme detection (2026, open source), plus
  Wawa-Lipsync for three.js rigs. We hold an unfair advantage: aivi
  lip-syncs **to its own TTS PCM**, which it has *before* the page
  does — visemes can be computed host-side and shipped as timed events
  with the audio, so the mouth is never late. Blink, gaze drift, a nod
  at the end of someone's turn: state machine, a few days.
- **Tier 2 — a full 3D avatar, live.** `met4citizen/TalkingHead`
  (MIT, browser JS): full-body 3D GLB avatars with ARKit/Oculus blend
  shapes, **real-time lip-sync from PCM**, emoji expressions, Mixamo
  animations — a 3D character that breathes, looks around, gestures and
  speaks, rendered in the same canvas we already stream. This is the
  sane ceiling of "fancy": days of integration, no new native code.
- **Tier 3 — a photoreal talking head (neural rendering).** The frontier
  with published numbers: Tavus Phoenix ~600 ms, Anam CARA-3 ~180 ms
  median at 25 fps/720×480 — those are *production* latencies, and the
  perceptual cliff is real: ~45 ms is the audio-vs-video mismatch
  threshold, and detection research says lip-sync drift is the #1 tell.
  On our MLX host, LivePortrait/SadTalker-class models at 25 fps is a
  research project, not an afternoon. Fun *later*; the wow is already
  banked at tier 1-2, where the face is clearly a character, clearly
  alive, and honestly a bot.

State is the soul regardless of tier: idle (blink, breathe), listening
(look toward whoever's speaking — we know from our own diarization),
thinking (the look-away), speaking (visemes), and a distinct *perked-up*
animation when someone says aivi's name. That, not polygon count, is
what makes people believe the thing is in the room.

These tiers are the spectrum of *human likeness*. The design ruling
below sits outside that spectrum entirely — and needs less machinery
than any tier here, not more.

## The face: a cute robot, clearly not human

The design ruling (the operator's): **a custom low-poly robot with big
eyes and a face that is a screen** — the screen shows what aivi is
*doing* (listening, thinking, speaking, working). A person avatar is out
on principle: *aivi is there to help, not replace you*, and a fake
person is threatening. That is not just taste — it is the literature:

- **Mori's uncanny valley**, and the 49-study meta-analysis of the
  Godspeed scales (Mara 2022): low-to-moderate human likeness → liking
  rises then levels; **high-but-not-perfect human likeness → responses
  flip negative**. Robots that are "more stylized, non-human (cartoon-like
  or clearly robotic) tend to be perceived more favorably" (design
  guidance converging on the same result); trust studies show faces
  inside the valley score *lower* trust than clearly-robotic ones.
- **Big eyes are the mechanism, not decoration**: the baby-schema
  effect (large eyes, round head) reliably pulls warmth and
  approach-motivation — the same reason Wall-E's Eve and BB-8 read as
  endearing. A clearly-robotic face with two big glossy eyes on a dark
  screen gets the disarm without the eeriness.
- And a practical bonus for a meeting bot: anthropomorphic-but-not-human
  machines also read as *less privacy-intrusive* in the same research
  line. A camera-equipped bot that looks like a little robot gets more
  benefit of the doubt than one that looks like a person.

**Why the screen-face is also the easy engineering.** A robot with a
display for a face needs none of the hard lip-sync machinery: the face
is a **2D canvas drawn as the model's emissive texture** (three.js: a
GLB robot, a face canvas as `emissiveMap` — the same canvas pipeline
that already feeds `captureStream`). State is UI, not muscles:

- **Speaking**: the mouth is a **waveform oscilloscope dancing to
  aivi's own PCM** — which aivi holds *before* the page ever plays it,
  so the mouth literally cannot be late. A waveform is also cuter and
  more honest than fake human lips on a robot.
- **Listening**: animated ear-bars / a level meter; and since our own
  diarization knows who talks, the face can show the **speaker's name**
  — "Bob" glows while Bob talks.
- **Thinking**: a patient little abacus or spinner; **confused**: a
  spiral question; **working on your request**: a tiny progress ticker.
- **Alive**: blink cycles, idle sway, a head-bob driven by its own
  audio energy, a **perk-up spring when someone says aivi's name**
  (our diarization hears it), sleepy dimming when nothing has happened
  for a while. All transforms and drawing — a state machine, no new
  native code.
- Eyes *can* still emote: two big morph-target blinks (eyes as blend
  shapes on the GLB) get surprise/joy/sleep for a few sliders.

Asset path: a commissioned low-poly aivi robot (one flat face-plane,
head + eye bones for bobbing), or start from CC0 robot packs
(Quaternius/Kenney robots, Sketchfab CC0) and dress it. The robot is
**aivi's brand asset**, the same way the soul file is its voice — one
design, every platform sees the same face because we draw it.

## Two languages, one character — at least Dutch and English

The mouth must speak **Dutch and English** with the same persisted
identity. The market moved in our favor:

| Engine | Dutch | English | Cloning/identity | License | Where it runs |

| **Chatterbox Multilingual** (Resemble AI, 0.5B) | ✅ in the 23+ language list, "same quality and controllability as English" | ✅ flagship | **zero-shot clone from ~5 s reference**, emotion dial (0.0–1.0), watermarked | **MIT** | PyTorch/CUDA; CPU real-time unproven — verify |
| **VibeVoice-Realtime-0.5B** (MLX fork) | experimental speaker added (DE FR IT JP KR **NL** PL PT ES), card says English-first, others "explore" | ✅ ~300 ms first audio, streams | `--save-voice` embeddings | MIT weights | MLX — the demo host |
| **Piper** | ✅ native Dutch voices (nl_NL) | ✅ many accents | fixed pretrained voices only, no cloning | MIT | any CPU, even a Pi |
| XTTS v2 (Coqui) | ✅ | ✅ | zero-shot clone | **CPML — non-commercial** | PyTorch; license bars shipping |
| **VibeVoice-ASR** (our ears) | ✅ among 10; ASR family auto-detects and **handles code-switching** mid-conversation | ✅ | — | MIT | MLX (mlx-audio lists it) |

The plan that follows from the table: **one persona, a per-language
voice table** — English on VibeVoice (streaming, the demo host), Dutch
via **Chatterbox's cloned voice** if it verifies on our hardware (MIT +
5-second cloning + Dutch + English in one model is exactly what we
need), with **Piper's Dutch voice as the always-works CPU fallback**.
A small accent difference between aivi's Dutch and its English is not
a bug — characters sound different across languages; it is *character*.
The ears are the easy half: VibeVoice-ASR **transcribes Dutch-English
code-switching** — which is precisely what a Dutch meeting with aivi
will actually be.

## The POC stack — assessed, and it is the solution to go with

The operator ran a working proof of the ears before any of this
(explored read-only 2026-10-04):

- **`speaker-id`** — the POC harness (~1.9k lines of Node 26,
  zero-dep but `jsonc-parser`: record / register / check / live /
  seed / split scripts over a `speaker-store.ts`). The code is
  genuinely careful: DB transactions, sha256 dedupe of enrollment
  audio, a **mislabel guard** (refuses a clip that matches another
  person's centroid better than its label's), per-person
  **quantile-calibrated** confidence (gates derived from each
  person's own leave-one-out genuine scores and margins, never one
  global threshold), a `sample-topk` strategy (average of the K
  strongest stored samples, always returns a ranked #1), and a live
  loop with a **never-build-latency rule** (if embedding falls
  behind real time, stale windows are dropped, newest kept). It is a
  harness, not a library — the parts to carry into aivi are the
  store schema (samples are already **versioned by model id** — an
  embedder swap is data, not migration) and the matching math.
- **`VibeVoice-Embed`** — not a third-party find: **the
  operator's own export** (`lemuriandezapada/VibeVoice-Embed` on HF,
  MIT). The VibeVoice-1.5B acoustic encoder lifted **bit-for-bit**
  into a standalone voice-embedding model: 344M params, 24 kHz in →
  one 64-d vector, deterministic (distribution mean, never a
  sample), >100× realtime, served by a self-contained
  OpenAI-compatible `/v1/embeddings` FastAPI server. Its own card
  measured it **on par with a purpose-trained embedder** (CosyVoice
  192-d): d′ **+3.61** / AUC **0.997** vs +3.47 / 0.990 — with the
  honest caveats that the set was synthetic, channel-matched TTS
  output, and **no metric gave a positive worst-case margin**:
  ranking and clustering, not one global accept/reject line.
- **`vibevoice.cpp`** — LocalAI's C++/ggml port of VibeVoice
  (MIT, `localai-org/vibevoice.cpp`, active): TTS **and**
  long-form **ASR with diarization** in one binary, CPU/CUDA/**Metal**/
  Vulkan, **no Python at inference**, a flat C ABI for embedding via
  dlopen, published pre-quantized bundles. Its 1.5B path clones a
  voice from a reference WAV at runtime; the realtime-0.5B path
  consumes pre-baked voice ggufs — exactly the `--save-voice`
  shape. And its test artifacts are quietly the best find:
  **`meeting-test.wav` + `transcript.json` — a real 30-minute Dutch
  council meeting, 8 speakers, diarized by VibeVoice-ASR.** That is
  a genuine validation set for everything speaker-related (and free
  proof of Dutch diarized ASR on real audio).

**The verdict, and the one test to run.** This stack is the spine —
for a reason beyond "it worked":

1. **The identity embedder is the same weights, as a separate
   model.** The operator's export lifted the acoustic encoder
   **bit-for-bit** — so identity has the *same lineage* as the ASR
   that aivi loads anyway (no separate training, no second voice
   story), but it is its own checkpoint and its own load: ~344M
   params, ~0.7 GB in bf16, >100× realtime, served as an
   OpenAI-compatible `/v1/embeddings`. Next to the ASR's ~13 GB that
   is a rounding error, not a second engine.
2. **aivi's architecture shrinks the embedder's known weakness.**
   The card's caveats (channel mismatch, no global threshold) are
   *open-set, cross-device* problems. aivi's real task is
   **2–10 known people**, where SFU per-track capture keeps each
   speaker's channel consistent within the meeting and the ring as
   teacher **enrolls in the same meeting's conditions**. Closed-set
   identification with same-meeting enrollment is the easy mode of
   speaker verification; the harness's per-person calibration and
   `sample-topk` are already the right shape for it.
3. **The A/B, cheap:** purpose-built embedders — **CAM++** (7.2M
   params) and **ERes2NetV2** (17.8M) — report ~0.6–0.8% EER on
   clean VoxCeleb1-O but **6–14% on 3D-Speaker cross-device trials**:
   on real audio the clean-set numbers evaporate for *everyone*,
   which is exactly the operator's untested caveat. Both are small
   ONNX exports (a few MB, CPU-milliseconds — vs the 344M encoder's
   ~2.7 GB in fp32), and LocalAI already packages them
   (`voice-detect.cpp`: ECAPA-TDNN, WeSpeaker, ERes2Net, CAM++).
   **The test:** `split-transcript.ts` already cuts the Dutch meeting
   into per-speaker clips with held-out samples — score CAM++ and
   ERes2NetV2 against VibeVoice-Embed on that real, diverse-channel
   set. The store versions vectors by model id, so if a small ONNX
   embedder wins on real audio, it is a drop-in, and the two can
   even run as a cheap ensemble.

**And it upgrades the Linux chapter:** `vibevoice.cpp` is
cross-platform with no Python — the same GGUF voice identity files,
Metal/CUDA/Vulkan/CPU from one binary, C ABI behind the sidecar's
stdio seam. Piper/Kokoro stay filed as the ultra-low-CPU fallback,
but the primary cross-platform voice story is now: *same GGUFs,
everywhere*.

What the POC does not have yet, honestly: the live path is RMS-gated
3-second windows over a *mixed* mic stream. The meeting shape is
richer, and the official toolchain now covers it:

- **Per-track, diarization is the transport's job.** Meet's SFU gives
  each participant their **own audio track**, so "who said this" is
  answered at capture — one streaming ASR per active track, or one
  on the merged stream with track-energy attribution; either way the
  diarizer is only needed for foreign audio (a recording, an OS-tap
  fallback), not for the live meeting.
- **Live transcription is official now: VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming**
  (microsoft/VibeVoice, `docs/vibevoice-asr-streaming.md`): emits
  text **per audio chunk** over a WebSocket, chunk size and
  lookahead baked into the checkpoint, greedy by default, and a
  **`--context_info` hotword channel** — which for a meeting bot is
  exactly right: feed it the attendee names, so "Sanne" transcribes
  as Sanne. Upstream verified on CUDA (with a CPU/MPS path in the
  demo); a ggml/MLX streaming port is the thing to verify.
- **Chunk-record while live-transcribing** (the operator's plan):
  keep the durable WAV per meeting *and* stream chunks to ASR +
  embedder as they arrive — so *who* and *what* are both live facts,
  not after-meeting facts.

### The scenario that ties it together — "email me a summary"

"aivi, email me a summary at the end of this meeting" — say it out
loud in the meeting, and every step is already in the plan:

1. **Hear it**: chunk ASR on that speaker's track emits the sentence;
   turn detector says finished; addressed? says yes (it heard
   "aivi").
2. **"Me" is an identification question — answered the speaker-id
   way, with its refusal modes intact.** Per-track capture buys
   *clean material*, not an answer: the sentence's audio is one
   person's voice, unmixed. Embed it, run the matching path the
   harness already implements — sample-topk over stored samples,
   per-person quantile calibration, minimum-sample rule. A
   **trusted match** (the harness's exit-0) resolves "me" to that
   person; person→email comes from the Calendar attendee roster.
   **Unknown or ambiguous is a legitimate outcome** (exit-1), not a
   failure: a ring correlation or attendee prior is a corroborator
   that *enrolls* a voice over time — it is never an identity a
   promise is addressed to. A confirmed answer to aivi's question
   *registers the voice on the spot* (the harness's
   `registerSpeakerSample`, mislabel guard included), so the store
   leaves every meeting richer.
3. **The todo for itself is a due job**: *at meeting end, compose a
   summary from the transcript, email it to dale@…* A job is exactly
   what aivi's todo list is made of. **Meeting end is not the
   calendar's end time** (the operator's correction): meetings run
   long, and the meeting is over when **everyone is gone** — the
   platform says so (participant count hits zero / the meeting-ended
   event). The scheduled end only arms a watcher, and the designed
   behavior at that watcher is kind: aivi **nudges the room that the
   meeting is over** — sometimes people need the reminder — then
   leaves when the last human does.
4. **Delivery is the email channel** (imapflow/nodemailer, already
   the next-cheapest channel), and the summary is one of the
   transcript's three designed consumers — follow-up work — so this
   scenario adds **no new machinery**. It is the whole meetings
   program in one sentence.
5. **No dictation in the meeting.** Following the operator's ruling
   (2026-10-05), aivi never asks someone to spell an email address
   aloud — we have the roster's addresses. What aivi cannot resolve
   or promises to follow up on becomes a **meeting question shared
   with the organizer** — live as it arises, or as end-of-meeting
   follow-ups — delivered in the organizer's **preferred channel**,
   a new Person concept ("where do you prefer being contacted").
   The organizer answers where they answer anyway; aivi's promise
   is kept without holding a human hostage in a meeting room.

### The people graph is already built — meetings extend it, not replace it

Checked against the code (2026-10-04): the host store already has
`people(id, name, email, roles)` with `aivi people create <name>
--email …` as an operator CLI, `channel_identities` binding a
platform account to a person (the link-code flow), and
`identityFor(channel, userId)` — "the person a channel account
belongs to, once linked; null while anonymous". Person tokens answer
*which person is this* for jobs and memory. Crucially, **the
operator can already add people who never use an aivi client**:
`people create` does not require minting a token (the token prompt
is a convenience), so a person can exist as pure record — the
colleague who only ever shows up in meetings and email. That is the
operator's requirement, and it is the current shape, not a wish.

What the meetings program adds is three extensions of the same
pattern, and one deliberate non-extension:

- **A third identity source, same shape.** Today a person is known
  by token and by channel account. Meetings add **voiceprint →
  person**: the voiceprint store's person key is the same
  `person_id`, bindings made exactly as channel bindings are —
  operator-confirmed, one binding per voice, unlinkable. A meeting
  speaker is then resolved like any channel speaker: source id →
  person, null while unknown.
- **Email graduates from remembered to load-bearing.** It is today
  "an email address to remember"; meetings make it **the join key**
  (ring hint → roster name → roster email → `person.email`), and
  the email channel (imapflow/nodemailer, already shortlisted) is
  where it pays: aivi can *read and respond* to a person's mail
  flow — summaries out, replies in, "forward me the deck" actions.
  What aivi may *do* with mail on whose behalf is a permission
  design, owned as its own work when the email channel is built —
  not smuggled in by a meetings demo.
- **A calendar per person, in the obvious sense.** The operator's
  calendar (the calendar role's adapter) says when aivi must be
  where; and because Calendar rosters carry **emails**, every
  meeting arrives with its people already half-resolved — attendees
  whose email matches `person.email` are known before the first
  word is spoken. That is also where the ring hint gets its
  confidence: a glowing "Dale" in a meeting whose roster has
  dale@… is a strong hint; a glowing name nobody recognizes is a
  new person waiting to be added.
- **Non-extension: persons are never auto-created.** A roster line
  or a ring name does not mint a person — the operator's `people
  create`, or the operator confirming a suggestion *into* an
  existing person, is what creates or binds. Unknown stays unknown
  (exactly `identityFor`'s null). The suggestion loop — clips +
  candidate + roster context sent to the operator — proposes; only
  the operator (or their confirmed reply) composes.
- **Identity lookup is code, not opinion** (the operator's ruling):
  "who is this voice" is a **tool call into the store** — matching
  math, calibrated gates, a fact or null. The language model never
  gets to guess a name; it gets to *ask the store* and must live
  with the answer, which is exactly `identityFor`'s discipline one
  sense further in.
- **The join gate**: aivi **only joins a meeting whose organizer is
  a known person** with the permission for it (a Person whose roles
  allow meetings, or the operator). An unknown organizer's calendar
  event is not a meeting to crash — it is nothing. This one rule
  settles a pile of problems at the door: the consent-announcement
  duty becomes a conversation with someone who invited aivi, the
  attendee roster is a known person's roster, and the whole program
  runs among people who chose to have aivi around.

So "email me a summary" resolves through machinery that exists:
voiceprint → person (extension), person.email → the "me" (already
there), the meeting's transcript → the summary's raw material
(already designed), a due job → delivery at meeting end (built
scheduler), email channel → the send (shortlisted next). The gaps
are exactly two new tables' worth of glue and one new channel —
which is why the whole scenario costs days of integration, not a
program.

### The voice dev lab can be Discord itself

Before any meeting platform (the operator's idea, 2026-10-05, and
the research says it works): aivi already lives in Discord as a
text channel — `@discordjs/voice` lets the same bot **join a server
voice channel, receive Opus per speaker and send its own**: ears,
VAD, streaming STT, turn detection, voiceprint matching, TTS and
barge-in, tested in a real conversation with the operator, zero
meeting platforms, zero DOM, and the latency number measured where
it will be lived. The honest caveats: **audio receive is
undocumented by Discord** ("stable support is not guaranteed"), E2EE
(DAVE) became mandatory March 2026 — `@snazzah/davey` ships

robot face cannot be tested here; those need the browser. Bots also
can't ring people: voice happens in a **server voice channel** the
operator and aivi both sit in, not a DM. Nail the voice part there
first; it is the cheapest full-loop lab there is. **Default off**
(the operator's ruling): the lab is a testing tool, used for a
session and then dark — aivi joins voice **only on invocation** (a
text-channel command, or the operator's own `VOICE_STATE_UPDATE`
arriving in that channel) and leaves when the session ends. No
idle voice connection, ever; "but it's lightweight" is no argument
when the machine already runs many parts and every cycle counts.

## Linux — the body never needed macOS

Asked the right question, this turned the design over in a good way.
The one macOS-flavoured piece was the **Core Audio tap for listening**
— and it turns out the browser body doesn't need an OS-level tap *at
all*:

- **Ears, OS-free.** The meeting's audio arrives in the page as
  **remote WebRTC tracks** (`RTCPeerConnection` `track` events — the
  standard receiving pattern every voice-AI WebRTC client uses). The
  same init script that installs the fake mic installs a fake *ear*:
  tap the remote stream into an `AudioWorklet`, ship PCM out over the
  websocket. This is strictly better than the OS tap — it hears the
  meeting, not the whole machine, it survives whatever the OS does,
  and **it is the same code on macOS, Linux and anything else Chrome
  runs on.** The OS tap (Core Audio taps; PulseAudio null-sink /
  PipeWire on Linux) is demoted to the native-desktop-app chapter,
  where it's needed and per-OS documented.
- **Mouth and face: never platform-specific.** An `AudioWorklet` mic
  and a `canvas.captureStream()` are page-side; Chrome's headless/new
  mode runs WebRTC fine on a Linux server (the open Meet bots ship on
  Linux), and CDP attach + `Page.startScreencast` are identical there.
  No ALSA/PipeWire configuration for the body at all — that was only
  ever the hermes-style *virtual-device* variant's cost, which we
  don't pay.
- **The Apple-only part is the voice engine, and the seam already
  isolates it.** The sidecar speaks framed stdio (PCM + small JSON);
  what answers behind it is per-host:
  - **macOS (the demo host):** MLX — VibeVoice voices (the persisted
    `.safetensors` embeddings), mlx-whisper/Parakeet ears.
  - **Linux, CPU:** the ONNX family — **Piper** (VITS-onnx TTS,
    real-time on plain CPU, MIT; a trained voice file is the identity,
    same idea as `--save-voice`) or **Kokoro** (`kokoro-js` runs in
    Node via onnxruntime); **whisper.cpp** or **faster-whisper**
    (CTranslate2) for ears; silero-VAD (ONNX) everywhere.
  - **Everywhere else (Linux, any accelerator):** `vibevoice.cpp` —
    the same VibeVoice as GGUF on CPU/CUDA/Vulkan with **no Python
    at inference** and a flat C ABI; the pre-baked voice ggufs are
    the *same voice identity*, portable because a voice is just
    tensors. Piper/Kokoro/whisper.cpp (ONNX) stay filed as the
    ultra-low-CPU fallback.
  The diarization/voiceprint system (the operator's, working) rides
  the same stdio seam; ONNX embeddings keep it cross-platform too.

So aivi-the-participant on a Linux server is: headless Chrome + the
init script + the sidecar on ONNX models. The demo host being Apple
Silicon with MLX is an acceleration, not a requirement — which is what
"aivi runs where you run" should mean.

## Preparedness — what could bite us, answered before the first line

Five open risks, researched to the point where starting is just
building.

### Who aivi *is* when it joins — the identity problem

Meet shows the **joining account's display name** in the participant
list. If aivi joins with the operator's Google account, aivi appears
as the operator — out. So aivi gets **its own Google account**,
display name `aivi`, signed into the `aivi_browser` profile like every
other browser identity. The 2026 field reality around it:

- Google now flags **all** meeting bots — including commercial
  notetakers — with a *"potential risks"* label in the waiting room
  (since late March 2026, Google-wide). Every bot pays this; a
  friendly-looking robot face is exactly the mitigation.
- Meeting access comes in three tiers: **Open** (link-holders join
  directly), **Trusted** (org members + calendar invitees; everyone
  else knocks), **Restricted** (invitees only). A bot account from
  another Workspace is *external* no matter what — org-only policies
  cannot be bypassed by bots, only relaxed by the host org.
- The knock expires: not admitted within ~5 minutes of the start, the
  bot is kicked (hermes calls it `lobby_timeout`).
- **The demo contract that follows**: demo meetings are **Open** (the
  lock icon offers "anyone can join") or aivi's account is a guest on
  the Calendar event — both admit aivi automatically. The operator's
  own meetings, where he is the host, can admit aivi once per meeting
  and that is fine too; it is strangers' Workspace meetings we can't
  auto-join, and we don't want to.

### The calendar trigger needs no public endpoint — pull first

Google Calendar push notifications require a **publicly accessible
HTTPS endpoint**, channels that expire and need renewal — and the
payloads are empty (a push only says "something changed"; you must
call back with a **syncToken** for the actual delta). Google's own
documentation then says: *"Notifications are not 100% reliable. Expect
a small percentage of messages to get dropped… make sure the
application still syncs even if no push messages are received."* So
the syncToken incremental sync is **mandatory anyway**, and push is
only an optimization on top. The design is decided by the docs:
a `calendar.sync` job polls incrementally (cheap — one call when
nothing changed), plus a one-shot timer armed for the next meeting's
start (a known instant, which our timer rule allows). Push via a
Pub/Sub relay is a later efficiency, not a precondition.

### When should aivi speak — the hardest question just got an open model

End-of-turn detection ("is the human *done*?") was "one of the
hardest open problems in voice AI since the first agents shipped" —
answer too early and aivi talks over people, too late and meetings
fill with dead air. As of 2026-09-22 there is an open-weights answer
we can run: **LiveKit Turn Detector v1** — 0.1B params (distilled from
Qwen2.5-7B into Qwen2.5-0.5B), **INT8 ONNX, CPU-only, <500 MB**, with
an npm package; it reads the **STT text of the last six turns** and
predicts completion (~300 ms budget). It supports **14 languages
including Dutch** — where its numbers are actually better than
English's. The aivi gate becomes a chain:

> VAD says *pause* → STT says the words → turn detector says *finished*
> → **addressed?** (voiceprint heard "aivi", or the LLM judges it was
> addressed) → speak.

Our echo-class self-interruption is structurally zero (the track-tap
ears never hear aivi's own TTS — the field's #1 self-interrupt cause).
What remains genuinely open is **addressee detection in multi-party
conversation** — knowing a sentence was for aivi and not for Bob; the
voiceprint name-hearing is our backstop, and full-duplex benchmarks
(Full-Duplex-Bench, LiveKit's open eot-bench) give us the way to
measure it. Latency budget, per the field's 300 ms rule: VAD tail +
turn check + LLM first token + VibeVoice's ~300 ms first audio —
target under ~1.5 s end to end, and *measure it on the first spike*.
The model's LiveKit Model License needs a terms read before shipping
(fine for research).

### The failure vocabulary is already written down

Hermes's issue tracker is a free map of this build's failure modes:
leave reasons `lobby_timeout` / `denied` / `page_closed` /
`duration_expired`; the muted-join → unmute-after-admission dance;
and the classic **"bot joins but remains silent"** class — mic muted
at join, or the PCM pump exiting before any audio arrived. The lesson
for our first spike: **assert on audio that arrived downstream** (the
page played it, a peer received it), never on bytes the host wrote.
Two more field facts: their barge-in is caption-derived and
**~500 ms late** ("the bot will talk over the first second or so of a
human interruption") — our PCM ears are structurally faster; and
**Meet uses only the first 2 channels** of an audio device (a
native-chapter quirk, noted for the day we need one).

### The law, Netherlands-first

- **Recording what you're part of is lawful in NL/EU** — there is
  settled practice that a participant may record their own
  conversation; it is not intercepting someone else's communication.
- **GDPR applies on top regardless** — a transcript is personal data:
  a lawful basis (legitimate interest is the honest one here), plus
  information, access and deletion duties. That makes the transcript
  retention question (owned by `docs/plans/knowledge-improvements.md`)
  a **legal** question, not hygiene.
- **Stricter neighbors are criminal, not civil**: Germany §201 StGB
  (up to 3 years) and France art. 226-1 criminalize recording private
  speech without consent *regardless of GDPR*. A Dutch demo is
  comfortable; a bot that joins EU-wide meetings must announce.
- And the line that decides our design: **no jurisdiction treats a
  bot merely being visible in the participant list as sufficient
  notice** — consent requires knowing *what* is recorded, *why*, and
  *how long*. aivi's join announcement (who I am, I'm transcribing,
  ask me to forget you) is load-bearing, not manners.

### Who is speaking — the fact is ours, the ring is a bonus

The operator's question: Meet draws a ring around the active
speaker's tile — do we get that signal? Three layers, best first:

- **The durable fact: our own ears.** The voiceprint system already
  labels every utterance, so *who is speaking right now* is a live
  fact aivi computes **everywhere** — the browser body, a Zoom RTMS
  adapter (which literally streams **per-participant audio and
  speaker-change notifications**, and diarized transcripts "not only
  what was said but who said it"), any future platform. It works for
  the participant with the **camera off**, and the labels persist
  across meetings. This is the source of truth in the plan already;
  the face showing "Bob" while Bob talks hangs off it.
- **The DOM ring: a free bonus signal, never the source.** Meet's
  active-speaker highlight is CSS on the tile; Recall's own docs say
  it's scrapable "but brittle as selectors will change." A
  MutationObserver on the tile costs nothing and gives a second
  opinion — a *when* signal (speech started, which tile) that agrees
  with our VAD — but nothing may depend on it surviving a Meet
  redesign, which is why attribution was never made the platform's.
- **The sanctioned version exists**: the Meet Media API's RTP header
  extensions include **Audio Level** per track; Zoom RTMS streams
  diarized transcripts and per-participant media. The field is
  converging on "active speaker as a first-class event" — which is
  why the meeting role should carry it as **`meeting.speaking`**: a
  channel event the core consumes, filled by voiceprint on the
  browser body, by RTMS events on Zoom, DOM ring only as a
  corroborator.

And where a **ring gets drawn**: injecting CSS into Meet's page would
only restyle *aivi's own render* — humans see their own clients, so a
ring there is visible only in aivi's screencast (its eyes). The real
places for the ring are the surfaces aivi owns: the **robot's face**
(the speaker's name glowing while they talk), and the **meeting
record page** in the dashboard — there the ring is just our DOM, made
of the `meeting.speaking` events streaming off the transcript.

**The ring as teacher — scoped honestly** (the operator's find, twice
over: the mechanism, then its boundary). The voiceprint store starts
knowing *voice 3*, not *Bob*. For **solo tracks** the pairing can
teach itself:

- Meet is an **SFU**: a participant with their own device arrives as
  a **separate audio track**. The in-page tap knows *which track had
  energy*; the tile ring is the same fact **with a display name on
  it** (and it exists for camera-off people too — their avatar tile
  lights, Google's own docs).
- **Correlate**: track X is loud exactly while the tile named "Bob"
  glows → a candidate enrollment of track X's voiceprint under Bob,
  confirmed by repetition across a few solo turns. Over time even a
  room speaker gets labeled this way: their voice shows up solo in
  *some* meeting somewhere, and the voiceprint carries it back into
  every room they later share a mic in.

**Where it breaks — rooms — and why that is integration work.**
Multiple people in one physical room share **one track and one
tile**: diarization separates their voices, but the ring can only
say *"Board Room is speaking"* — it cannot attribute voice 1 vs
voice 2 inside that tile. What makes a room a room (device vs room
tile, how the platform names and merges participants, when tracks
are shared) is exactly the **platform knowledge the meeting adapter
owns** — the same layer that knows which selector is the ring. This
is the integration part, per platform, and it is why the ring was
never allowed to be load-bearing for identity.

**Unknown voices are the normal case, not the edge** — anyone can
invite anyone, so every meeting will hold voices the store has
never heard. For those, the ring demotes gracefully from teacher to
**hint**: *store the ring name seen while an unrecognized voice
spoke* — a candidate label with a confidence, never an identity.

**Then labeling becomes confirmation, not assignment** (the
operator's design): aivi sends the operator — over the channels it
already has — the unknown voice's **audio clips with suggestions**
("this might have been Dale — the ring said so, here are three
clips"), and the operator confirms or corrects in seconds. The
confirmed clip enrolls on the spot (the harness's
`registerSpeakerSample`, mislabel guard included). Each meeting
leaves the store richer, and no human ever does bookkeeping.

**The join key is the email, not the name.** A display name is a
label — two Bobs, a "Dave" who signs "D.", a room called "Board
Room". What links a speaker to a *person* is the participant info
behind the name: the Calendar roster carries **name + email**, the
email resolves to the people database, and the email is what
"email me a summary" needs anyway. So the store's person record is
keyed by email; the ring's display name is matched against the
roster to *propose* that link (and against nothing, and stays a
hint, when the roster has no such name — the invited-guest case).

### And one sanctioned door Google opened

The **Meet Media API** (developer preview) now streams *live* RTP
audio/video to a bot that joins via Google's own path — the Meet
equivalent of Zoom's RTMS. The price: restricted OAuth scopes
(app verification + a 4–7 week third-party security assessment), the
Developer Preview terms bar public apps, a Workspace admin must
consent, and **participants are informed Meet API is in use**. Filed:
this is the *shipping* chapter for Meet (the Zoom-RTMS shape), not
the demo chapter — but it says where the platform is going, and our
seats-in-a-meeting/roles design is the same either way.

## The fine print

- **The one custom native piece, if ever**: a **system-wide** virtual
  camera a *native* meeting app can pick (Zoom desktop, Teams desktop)
  is an Apple **CMIO Camera Extension** — Apple's
  `SampleCameraExtension` is the sanctioned pattern (signed Swift app;
  the OBS virtual camera is one). The browser body needs none of it;
  this is the "and it works in the desktop app too" chapter, filed for
  later.
- **Echo is the known killer**: Zoom's forum documents a translated-voice
  bot whose output re-entered through participants' mics and self-sustained.
  The track tap answers it by construction: aivi's ears hear only the
  **remote tracks** — the other participants — and never its own PCM,
  which goes straight from the pump into the page's mic. Barge-in on VAD
  still applies: aivi stops mid-word when a human talks.
- **Consent is a feature**: jurisdictions require notice a bot records;
  Meet shows no bot badge — so aivi announces itself in the meeting chat
  on join, in its own voice if the operator likes. The `_worker: aivi_`
  signature habit already makes this natural.
- **Which ToS, spelled out.** Two different things hide behind "ToS":
  **Google's Terms of Service** (the Automated Systems clause: no
  access by scripts/bots without written permission) — its realistic
  worst case is **enforcement, not law**: Meet does bot-detection and
  the field's mitigation is exactly aivi's shape, a real signed-in
  Chrome over CDP, not a headless signature (puppeteer-extra #334 is
  the receipt). And aivi's browser plugin already drives that same
  Chrome under the operator's account for everything else — a meeting
  join adds no new category of automation. The heavier duty is not a
