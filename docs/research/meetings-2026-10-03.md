# Meetings: aivi as a participant — the body plan

Research 2026-10-03 (web sources as of this date), refined 2026-10-03 after
the operator's rulings: **Google Meet and Google Calendar go first** —
that's what the demo audience runs, and the demo is the point — the
**body is aivi's own virtual camera and virtual microphone with an audio
tap for listening**, and the design must serve **more than one meeting
platform** the way forge/tracker serve more than one code host.

## The body, in one paragraph

aivi does not scrape a meeting page and it does not beg each platform for
API permission. **aivi owns one real Chrome — the persistent
`aivi_browser` Chrome it already keeps — and that Chrome has a face and
a voice.** An init script, installed before any meeting page loads,
replaces the page's media devices: `getUserMedia` returns aivi's
streams (PCM out of the voice engine for the microphone, an animated
canvas for the camera), and the meeting's incoming audio — the remote
WebRTC tracks — is tapped in-page and streamed back for local
speech-to-text. No operating-system audio anywhere; the same code runs
on macOS and Linux. The meeting platforms see an ordinary participant;
what makes it aivi is behind the two streams.
Because the trick is below the platform, **one body works on every
platform with a web client** — Meet, Teams, Zoom, Webex, Jitsi — and a
platform adapter shrinks to: open this URL, click join, tell me
who's here, and stream me what the tab shows.

## Why this is the logical shape (and what it rests on)

1. **The mic-injection pattern is in production, in the open.** Several
   shipped projects do exactly this: the meeting page records from a
   fake/virtual microphone and a pump streams TTS PCM into it.
   `CoderOMaster/meeting-assistant` injects bot audio **directly into
   Chrome's mic stream** — in-page, with interruption handling and echo
   detection ("bot stops mid-sentence when a human speaks"), which is
   the whole hard part, already solved once. `NousResearch/hermes-agent`
   ships a Google Meet plugin end to end (Playwright joins, speaks a TTS
   PCM stream, auto-unmutes when Meet seats it muted) — and its issue
   tracker documents the live failure modes (joined-but-muted, the pump
   exiting early) for free.
2. **aivi already keeps the Chrome.** The browser plugin is one
   persistent Chrome for unattended sessions and shared logins, and
   Playwright's `connectOverCDP` attaches to an already-running Chrome
   with a signed-in profile — the open `screenappai/meeting-bot`
   (MIT, joins Meet/Teams/Zoom) supports exactly that attach mode. The
   signed-in real Chrome is also the field's answer to Meet's
   bot-detection (puppeteer-extra #334): a real profile over CDP, not a
   fresh headless signature.
3. **Listening is a track tap, not an OS tap.** The meeting's audio
   arrives in the page as **remote WebRTC tracks** — every voice-AI
   WebRTC client receives them the same way (`RTCPeerConnection`
   `track` events). Tap them into an `AudioWorklet`, stream PCM to the
   host, done. It hears the meeting and not the machine, it is the
   same code on every OS, and it needs no permission the browser page
   hasn't already been given. (An OS-level tap — macOS **Core Audio
   taps** (`sparticleinc/mac-audio-capture` npm; `AudioTee` the
   reference), Linux null-sink/PipeWire — exists and is filed for the
   one chapter that needs it: a *native* meeting app, below.)
4. **The in-page override needs nothing installed.** Before the page
   loads, an init script (`Page.addScriptToEvaluateOnNewDocument` /
   Playwright `addInitScript`) replaces `navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia`:
   audio returns a `MediaStream` from an `AudioContext` whose source is
   a WS-fed `AudioWorklet`; video returns `canvas.captureStream()`. The
   meeting page — Meet included — accepts them as its devices. This is
   the demo's whole media surface: **zero system installs, pure CDP.**

## The voice: ears and mouth, local, with a persistent identity

The host is Apple Silicon; MLX already serves aivi's models (the operator
has `mlx-serve` up). The audio stack has an all-local shape
(`mlx-audio` / `mlx-speech` / the VibeVoice MLX forks):

- **Mouth — VibeVoice.** MIT weights: 1.5B and 7B, plus
  **VibeVoice-Realtime-0.5B**, the streaming variant built for
  conversation latency. The operator's find — persisted voice identity —
  is first-class in the toolchain: encode a reference clip once
  (`--save-voice` → a `.safetensors` voice embedding) and synthesize
  with `--voice` after: bit-identical output, ~100 ms prefill (the
  EmZod fork caches the per-voice prefix KV and boots from a pre-quantized
  snapshot in ~3 s, and ships a **framed-stdio inference worker** built
  for a resident audio daemon — the exact sidecar shape). Voice files
  live in the home beside `.env`, 0600, never in config. Kokoro-82M is
  the instant fallback voice while VibeVoice warms. Languages — the
  mouth must speak Dutch as well as English — get their own section
  below.
- **Ears — local STT, streaming, with our own voice labels.**
  `mlx-whisper` or `parakeet-mlx` (fast, on-device) behind a silero-VAD
  (ONNX) segmenter. Speaker attribution is **ours, already working**
  (the operator's): diarization separates voices, a couple of manual
  assignments enroll a voiceprint, and the system remembers — the same
  voices are recognized in every later meeting. The labels survive any
  platform redesign because they were never the platform's.
- **Eyes.** A screen share is a rendered video track; **CDP tab
  screencast** (`Page.startScreencast`, a steady JPEG stream of the
  Meet tab) or a changed-what screenshot feeds a vision model — local
  MLX vision, a cloud one as an adapter. "Look at this chart" works,
  and shared frames land in the meeting record beside the transcript.
- **Plumbing.** MLX is Python, aivi is Node: a **sidecar child process
  over framed stdio** — PCM and small JSON in, PCM out — the same shape
  as the CLI's exec relay. The host never loads a model into its own
  process.

## Google first — the demo's pieces

**Calendar (Google Calendar API first).** `googleapis` npm; OAuth once,
refresh token in the home like every other secret. The live question —
"what's my next meeting", "join the 3pm" — is `events.list` with a
timeMin; the *reactive* answer is **push watch channels** (a channel
renews itself; renewal is a due job, not a timer — the no-polling rule
holds). `onlineMeetings: {conferenceData}` carries the join URL. Later,
the same role answers Microsoft Graph (`onlineMeetings` + webhook
subscriptions) and plain **CalDAV via `tsdav`** (polling with ctag —
which is a system job like `projects-sync`, so still no new timers).

**Meet (browser adapter, the first meeting adapter).** The flow, all
over CDP against the persistent Chrome:

1. `connectOverCDP` → new tab → the `meet.google.com/xxx` URL.
2. Init script installs the mic and camera streams (above).
3. Click join (the pre-join screen's "Join now"); Meet seats guests
   muted — `hermes` documents the unmute-after-admission dance.
4. **Listen — and label the voices ourselves. No captions.** The
   remote-track tap ships the meeting's PCM to the host; VAD segments
   the speech, local STT words it. Attribution is
   **our own voice-labelling** (built and working, the operator's):
   diarization separates the speakers, a **couple of manual
   assignments** — "that's Alice" — enroll a voiceprint, and the
   system remembers; every later meeting recognizes the same voices.
   No dependence on Meet's caption DOM, nothing to break when Meet
   redesigns, and the labels are ours to keep across meetings.
5. **Eyes.** In a call, a screen share is a video track; the page
   renders it. The cheap, robust way to *see* it: **CDP screencast of
   the tab** (`Page.startScreencast`, a steady JPEG stream of what
   Meet shows — the shared slide, the shared face) or a periodic tab
   screenshot when something changes, fed to a vision model (local
   MLX vision, or a cloud one as an adapter). Eyes on the meeting
   means: "aivi, look at this chart" works, the shared screen lands
   in the meeting record next to the transcript, and frames OCR what
   people put on screen instead of what they said. If Meet ever
   renders shared text as on-screen captions, our diarized audio plus
   frame OCR label it — an opportunistic extra, never a dependence.
6. **Speak**: text → VibeVoice worker → PCM over the WS → AudioWorklet →
   the mic stream. **Barge-in**: VAD on the tap pauses the pump mid-word
   when a human speaks (the meeting-assistant reference's behavior).
7. **Face**: the aivi robot — a low-poly GLB whose screen-face is a 2D
   canvas drawn as its emissive texture (state, eyes, a waveform mouth
   dancing to its own PCM), handed to the page as the camera stream.
   The webcam is free once the mic works, and it is half the wow.
   The design section below owns the details.
8. **Announce**: post "aivi is here, recording with its own voice —
   say 'aivi, …' to ask it something" to the meeting chat on join.
   Consent as a feature, never a footnote.

**The wow demo, end to end**: the 3pm is on the calendar → aivi joins on
time with its face and its persisted voice → the meeting talks; nobody
notices the mic is a worklet → someone says "aivi, what did we decide
about the schema?" → it answers *in the meeting*, in voice → after the
call the transcript lands in the conversation, dreaming turns the
decisions into `facts.md`, and the next morning aivi can say what
yesterday's meeting decided. The memory half is free: see roles.

**Transcripts are their own thing — a first-class artifact, not a
by-product** (ruled 2026-10-03). Each meeting keeps its record under
the home (one directory per meeting: the transcript, the participant
list, the calendar event it came from), and it is **not** dreaming's
throwaway scratch — dreaming's own transcripts are ephemeral; a
meeting's transcript is durable, the meeting's asset. Three consumers,
in order of nearness:

1. **In the meeting and right after**: aivi answers "what did we say
   about X?" from the transcript itself — exact words, attributed,
   quotable — not from a summary of a summary.
2. **Follow-up work reads it directly**: the action items, decisions
   and open questions in a transcript are what a post-meeting job turns
   into tickets (a tracker lane!), summary messages to participants,
   reminder jobs with due dates. The transcript is the source of truth
   the follow-up quotes, the same way a run's ledger is.
3. **Dreaming reviews it** along with conversations, so meeting
   decisions become `facts.md` and proposals like anything else said.

The meeting directory is a natural **knowledge source** — the `conversation`
kind already exists — so `aivi_search` reaches transcripts the same way
it reaches everything else said, with no new index machinery.

And the channel-events shape still holds *while the meeting runs*: the
live transcript arrives as channel-shaped lines (origin `meet`, the
meeting id as the channel, speaker-attributed), so the engine, identity
and commands ride along during the call. The durable transcript beside
it is what outlives the call.

## The generic roles — core spells them, adapters fill them

The forge/tracker move, one layer up. Nothing upstream says "Google".

| Role | What it answers | Adapters, in build order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **calendar** | when and where: upcoming events, join URLs, free/busy, "a meeting is starting" | `google` (API + watch channels) → `ms-graph` → `caldav` (`tsdav`, the suite-agnostic floor: Fastmail/Apple/Nextcloud/Google) |
| **meeting** | presence: join(url), transcript of who-said-what, say(text), leave, participants, what's on screen | `meet` (browser) → `teams-web` / `zoom-web` / `webex` / `jitsi` (same body, per-platform join and tab reader) → `zoom-rtms` (Zoom's official **Node.js Real-Time Media Streams SDK**: sanctioned live per-participant audio/video/transcripts over webhooks — the native door when a demo needs no browser) |
| **voice** | ears and mouth: stt(pcm)→text with speaker segments; tts(text, voiceId)→pcm | MLX sidecar (VibeVoice voices, whisper/parakeet ears) → a cloud STT/TTS as just another adapter |
| **channel** *(built)* | conversation: a meeting is a conversation **without threads** | the meeting transcript arrives as channel-shaped events — origin `meet`, the meeting id as the channel, speaker-attributed user lines |

The payoff line: a meeting arriving as channel events gets the
**ChannelEngine, link/identity, chat commands, and dreaming for free**.
"aivi knew the meeting happened and what was decided" needs no new
memory machinery — only the adapter's transcript emitter and the
meeting's durable transcript beside it.

Platform notes for the columns after Meet:

- **Zoom** is the friendly native one: official **RTMS Node SDK**
  (`rtms-quickstart-js`) delivers live per-participant audio, video,
  transcripts and events via webhooks; since 2026-03 cross-account joins
  require an authorized app (ZAK/OBF/RTMS). Raw audio **send** needs the
  native Meeting SDK (macOS/Windows/Linux C++) — or the body works on
  zoom.us web like it works on Meet.
- **Teams**' sanctioned live-media bot is .NET-on-Windows only
  (Microsoft Q&A confirms: no Node, REST or WebSocket path, no roadmap);
  Graph gives post-meeting transcripts in any language; the browser body
  is what every commercial Teams notetaker actually does during a call.
- **Webex** has a community Node SDK and a documented real-time media
  path through the browser SDK (Cisco's own blog builds a meeting bot
  that joins and reads media streams) — a fine third column.
- **Jitsi Meet** is the self-hosted dream: Apache-2.0, `lib-jitsi-meet`
  in Node joins a room as a participant with full track control — the
  body needs no override there, it *is* a participant. If a target
  company runs anything self-hosted, it's this.

## Build order

1. **Calendar adapter (Google)** + a `calendar.watch` system job —
   aivi knows meetings are coming. No media. A day.
2. **Meet join + listen**: CDP body, tap → VAD → STT, our voiceprint
   labels → transcript as channel events into the engine. The first "what did we decide?"
   moment. This is the demo's spine.
3. **Speak + barge-in**: VibeVoice worker, WS-fed AudioWorklet mic,
   VAD-pause. The wow lands here.
4. **Face**: the robot — a CC0 low-poly GLB with a canvas-drawn screen
   face (big eyes, state glyphs) behind `captureStream`, then the
   living layer: waveform mouth from its own PCM, blink cycles, the
   perk-up. A day for the first face on screen, days for the soul.
5. **Email channel** (`imapflow`/`mailparser`/`nodemailer`) — still the
   cheapest new channel overall and the identity bridge for meeting
   strangers (reply-to-aivi finds the transcript); the demo can wave it
   in later.
6. **Zoom adapter (RTMS)** — proves the meeting role on a sanctioned
   API, same seams, no browser.
7. Teams-web / Webex / Jitsi adapters as the demo circuit demands.

## How fancy can the face get

The face is a canvas; anything a canvas can draw, Meet can see. The
tiers, cheapest first — all of them drive the *same* `captureStream()`:

- **Tier 0 — the honest mark.** A drawn sigil or portrait that breathes
  (a slow scale/opacity cycle) and shifts colour by state: listening,
  thinking, speaking. An afternoon. Already convincing in its own way:
  it says *I am aivi* rather than trying to pass.
- **Tier 1 — a rigged 2D puppet with real lip-sync.** Audio-driven
  visemes in the browser are a solved, tiny thing: Meta's Oculus
  viseme set (15 visemes) has ONNX ports, and there are zero-dependency
  ~15 KB engines that make **any 2D browser avatar talk from streaming
  audio** with real-time viseme detection (2026, open source), plus
  Wawa-Lipsync for three.js rigs. We hold an unfair advantage: aivi
  lip-syncs **to its own TTS PCM**, which it has *before* the page
  does — visemes can be computed host-side and shipped as timed events
  with the audio, so the mouth is never late. Blink, gaze drift, a nod
  at the end of someone's turn: state machine, a few days.
- **Tier 2 — a full 3D avatar, live.** `met4citizen/TalkingHead`
  (MIT, browser JS): full-body 3D GLB avatars with ARKit/Oculus blend
  shapes, **real-time lip-sync from PCM**, emoji expressions, Mixamo
  animations — a 3D character that breathes, looks around, gestures and
  speaks, rendered in the same canvas we already stream. This is the
  sane ceiling of "fancy": days of integration, no new native code.
- **Tier 3 — a photoreal talking head (neural rendering).** The frontier
  with published numbers: Tavus Phoenix ~600 ms, Anam CARA-3 ~180 ms
  median at 25 fps/720×480 — those are *production* latencies, and the
  perceptual cliff is real: ~45 ms is the audio-vs-video mismatch
  threshold, and detection research says lip-sync drift is the #1 tell.
  On our MLX host, LivePortrait/SadTalker-class models at 25 fps is a
  research project, not an afternoon. Fun *later*; the wow is already
  banked at tier 1-2, where the face is clearly a character, clearly
  alive, and honestly a bot.

State is the soul regardless of tier: idle (blink, breathe), listening
(look toward whoever's speaking — we know from our own diarization),
thinking (the look-away), speaking (visemes), and a distinct *perked-up*
animation when someone says aivi's name. That, not polygon count, is
what makes people believe the thing is in the room.

These tiers are the spectrum of *human likeness*. The design ruling
below sits outside that spectrum entirely — and needs less machinery
than any tier here, not more.

## The face: a cute robot, clearly not human

The design ruling (the operator's): **a custom low-poly robot with big
eyes and a face that is a screen** — the screen shows what aivi is
*doing* (listening, thinking, speaking, working). A person avatar is out
on principle: *aivi is there to help, not replace you*, and a fake
person is threatening. That is not just taste — it is the literature:

- **Mori's uncanny valley**, and the 49-study meta-analysis of the
  Godspeed scales (Mara 2022): low-to-moderate human likeness → liking
  rises then levels; **high-but-not-perfect human likeness → responses
  flip negative**. Robots that are "more stylized, non-human (cartoon-like
  or clearly robotic) tend to be perceived more favorably" (design
  guidance converging on the same result); trust studies show faces
  inside the valley score *lower* trust than clearly-robotic ones.
- **Big eyes are the mechanism, not decoration**: the baby-schema
  effect (large eyes, round head) reliably pulls warmth and
  approach-motivation — the same reason Wall-E's Eve and BB-8 read as
  endearing. A clearly-robotic face with two big glossy eyes on a dark
  screen gets the disarm without the eeriness.
- And a practical bonus for a meeting bot: anthropomorphic-but-not-human
  machines also read as *less privacy-intrusive* in the same research
  line. A camera-equipped bot that looks like a little robot gets more
  benefit of the doubt than one that looks like a person.

**Why the screen-face is also the easy engineering.** A robot with a
display for a face needs none of the hard lip-sync machinery: the face
is a **2D canvas drawn as the model's emissive texture** (three.js: a
GLB robot, a face canvas as `emissiveMap` — the same canvas pipeline
that already feeds `captureStream`). State is UI, not muscles:

- **Speaking**: the mouth is a **waveform oscilloscope dancing to
  aivi's own PCM** — which aivi holds *before* the page ever plays it,
  so the mouth literally cannot be late. A waveform is also cuter and
  more honest than fake human lips on a robot.
- **Listening**: animated ear-bars / a level meter; and since our own
  diarization knows who talks, the face can show the **speaker's name**
  — "Bob" glows while Bob talks.
- **Thinking**: a patient little abacus or spinner; **confused**: a
  spiral question; **working on your request**: a tiny progress ticker.
- **Alive**: blink cycles, idle sway, a head-bob driven by its own
  audio energy, a **perk-up spring when someone says aivi's name**
  (our diarization hears it), sleepy dimming when nothing has happened
  for a while. All transforms and drawing — a state machine, no new
  native code.
- Eyes *can* still emote: two big morph-target blinks (eyes as blend
  shapes on the GLB) get surprise/joy/sleep for a few sliders.

Asset path: a commissioned low-poly aivi robot (one flat face-plane,
head + eye bones for bobbing), or start from CC0 robot packs
(Quaternius/Kenney robots, Sketchfab CC0) and dress it. The robot is
**aivi's brand asset**, the same way the soul file is its voice — one
design, every platform sees the same face because we draw it.

## Two languages, one character — at least Dutch and English

The mouth must speak **Dutch and English** with the same persisted
identity. The market moved in our favor:

| Engine | Dutch | English | Cloning/identity | License | Where it runs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Chatterbox Multilingual** (Resemble AI, 0.5B) | ✅ in the 23+ language list, "same quality and controllability as English" | ✅ flagship | **zero-shot clone from ~5 s reference**, emotion dial (0.0–1.0), watermarked | **MIT** | PyTorch/CUDA; CPU real-time unproven — verify |
| **VibeVoice-Realtime-0.5B** (MLX fork) | experimental speaker added (DE FR IT JP KR **NL** PL PT ES), card says English-first, others "explore" | ✅ ~300 ms first audio, streams | `--save-voice` embeddings | MIT weights | MLX — the demo host |
| **Piper** | ✅ native Dutch voices (nl_NL) | ✅ many accents | fixed pretrained voices only, no cloning | MIT | any CPU, even a Pi |
| XTTS v2 (Coqui) | ✅ | ✅ | zero-shot clone | **CPML — non-commercial** | PyTorch; license bars shipping |
| **VibeVoice-ASR** (our ears) | ✅ among 10; ASR family auto-detects and **handles code-switching** mid-conversation | ✅ | — | MIT | MLX (mlx-audio lists it) |

The plan that follows from the table: **one persona, a per-language
voice table** — English on VibeVoice (streaming, the demo host), Dutch
via **Chatterbox's cloned voice** if it verifies on our hardware (MIT +
5-second cloning + Dutch + English in one model is exactly what we
need), with **Piper's Dutch voice as the always-works CPU fallback**.
A small accent difference between aivi's Dutch and its English is not
a bug — characters sound different across languages; it is *character*.
The ears are the easy half: VibeVoice-ASR **transcribes Dutch-English
code-switching** — which is precisely what a Dutch meeting with aivi
will actually be.

## Linux — the body never needed macOS

Asked the right question, this turned the design over in a good way.
The one macOS-flavoured piece was the **Core Audio tap for listening**
— and it turns out the browser body doesn't need an OS-level tap *at
all*:

- **Ears, OS-free.** The meeting's audio arrives in the page as
  **remote WebRTC tracks** (`RTCPeerConnection` `track` events — the
  standard receiving pattern every voice-AI WebRTC client uses). The
  same init script that installs the fake mic installs a fake *ear*:
  tap the remote stream into an `AudioWorklet`, ship PCM out over the
  websocket. This is strictly better than the OS tap — it hears the
  meeting, not the whole machine, it survives whatever the OS does,
  and **it is the same code on macOS, Linux and anything else Chrome
  runs on.** The OS tap (Core Audio taps; PulseAudio null-sink /
  PipeWire on Linux) is demoted to the native-desktop-app chapter,
  where it's needed and per-OS documented.
- **Mouth and face: never platform-specific.** An `AudioWorklet` mic
  and a `canvas.captureStream()` are page-side; Chrome's headless/new
  mode runs WebRTC fine on a Linux server (the open Meet bots ship on
  Linux), and CDP attach + `Page.startScreencast` are identical there.
  No ALSA/PipeWire configuration for the body at all — that was only
  ever the hermes-style *virtual-device* variant's cost, which we
  don't pay.
- **The Apple-only part is the voice engine, and the seam already
  isolates it.** The sidecar speaks framed stdio (PCM + small JSON);
  what answers behind it is per-host:
  - **macOS (the demo host):** MLX — VibeVoice voices (the persisted
    `.safetensors` embeddings), mlx-whisper/Parakeet ears.
  - **Linux, CPU:** the ONNX family — **Piper** (VITS-onnx TTS,
    real-time on plain CPU, MIT; a trained voice file is the identity,
    same idea as `--save-voice`) or **Kokoro** (`kokoro-js` runs in
    Node via onnxruntime); **whisper.cpp** or **faster-whisper**
    (CTranslate2) for ears; silero-VAD (ONNX) everywhere.
  - **Linux, NVIDIA GPU:** the official `microsoft/VibeVoice`
    (PyTorch/CUDA) or GGUF quants — the *same voice identity*,
    portable, because a voiceprint embedding is just tensors.
  The diarization/voiceprint system (the operator's, working) rides
  the same stdio seam; ONNX embeddings keep it cross-platform too.

So aivi-the-participant on a Linux server is: headless Chrome + the
init script + the sidecar on ONNX models. The demo host being Apple
Silicon with MLX is an acceleration, not a requirement — which is what
"aivi runs where you run" should mean.

## The fine print

- **The one custom native piece, if ever**: a **system-wide** virtual
  camera a *native* meeting app can pick (Zoom desktop, Teams desktop)
  is an Apple **CMIO Camera Extension** — Apple's
  `SampleCameraExtension` is the sanctioned pattern (signed Swift app;
  the OBS virtual camera is one). The browser body needs none of it;
  this is the "and it works in the desktop app too" chapter, filed for
  later.
- **Echo is the known killer**: Zoom's forum documents a translated-voice
  bot whose output re-entered through participants' mics and self-sustained.
  The track tap answers it by construction: aivi's ears hear only the
  **remote tracks** — the other participants — and never its own PCM,
  which goes straight from the pump into the page's mic. Barge-in on VAD
  still applies: aivi stops mid-word when a human talks.
- **Consent is a feature**: jurisdictions require notice a bot records;
  Meet shows no bot badge — so aivi announces itself in the meeting chat
  on join, in its own voice if the operator likes. The `_worker: aivi_`
  signature habit already makes this natural.
- **Which ToS, spelled out.** Two different things hide behind "ToS":
  **Google's Terms of Service** (the Automated Systems clause: no
  access by scripts/bots without written permission) — its realistic
  worst case is **enforcement, not law**: Meet does bot-detection and
  the field's mitigation is exactly aivi's shape, a real signed-in
  Chrome over CDP, not a headless signature (puppeteer-extra #334 is
  the receipt). And aivi's browser plugin already drives that same
  Chrome under the operator's account for everything else — a meeting
  join adds no new category of automation. The heavier duty is not a
  ToS at all: **meetings have people in them.** Meet shows no badge
  for a participant, and in two-party-consent jurisdictions recording
  undisclosed is *illegal* — so aivi announcing itself on join is not
  politeness, it is the legal shape of the feature. Separately, the
  **vendor API ToS** (Recall.ai et al bar training competing models)
  is one more reason the body is ours, not rented. Zoom (RTMS) and
  Teams (Graph, post-meeting) remain the authorized doors for installs
  that must be sanctioned; the demo runs on the operator's own machine
  and account, the honest ground to start from.
- **No new timers, again**: calendar watch renewal, join-at-time, and
  post-meeting processing are all due jobs — the scheduler already
  runs to the next due instant.
